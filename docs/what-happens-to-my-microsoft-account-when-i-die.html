What happens to my Microsoft account — Outlook, OneDrive, Xbox — when I die?

FULLY SOURCED

Nothing is deleted right away, and Microsoft is unusually candid that it does not want to be the one deciding who gets in. Its own support page opens: "For privacy and other legal reasons, we are generally unable to provide information to non-account holders." Source

If whoever is handling the estate has the login, Microsoft's guidance is simple: sign in and close the account, the same as anyone would for any other reason — no notification to Microsoft required. If nobody has the login, Microsoft describes a slow, automatic wind-down instead of a request process: "Outlook.com and OneDrive accounts will be frozen after 1 year and any email messages and files stored on OneDrive will be deleted shortly after. Microsoft accounts expire after two (2) years of inactivity." So a family that does nothing at all eventually loses everything in the account — mail, files, photos — without ever having to ask.

To get in sooner, or to get content out before that automatic deletion, the bar is the highest of any platform in our whole set so far: "Microsoft must first be formally served with a valid subpoena or court order to consider whether it is able to lawfully release a deceased or incapacitated user's information regarding a personal email account... OneDrive storage, or any other aspect of their Microsoft account." A death certificate and executor paperwork — enough for PayPal, Venmo, or LinkedIn — is not enough here; Microsoft states it "will only respond to non-criminal subpoenas and court orders served on Microsoft's registered agent in the requesting party's state or region and is unable to respond to faxed or emailed requests for such matters." A subpoena still doesn't guarantee anything: "sending a request or providing a subpoena or court order does not guarantee that we will be able to assist you."

The page documents two narrow exceptions to the subpoena requirement, both outside the U.S. In Germany, customer support can grant access with a death certificate, requestor ID, and "a copy of certificate of inheritance (Erbschein) or other documentation from a court that proves sole heirship... or a signed power of attorney." In China, support can grant access to a documented "close relative" (spouse, parent, child, sibling, grandparent, or grandchild) with equivalent proof. No such documentation-only path exists for U.S. customers — a subpoena or court order is the only route.

What we could not verify: how long Microsoft's subpoena review typically takes once served, and whether the "two years of inactivity" auto-expiration timer is affected by having previously requested data through the subpoena process — the page describes each mechanism separately, not how they interact.
