What happens to your digital life when you die — 46 questions, answered from primary sources

Forty-six questions people actually ask about death and online accounts, each answered once, properly, with a link to the page the answer came from. Every claim was checked against the vendor's, regulator's, or legislature's own documentation — questions 1–15 on 2–3 August 2026, questions 16–30 on 6–7 August 2026, and questions 31–46 on 7–8 August 2026. Where we could not verify something, the answer says so instead of guessing.

Each answer is written to be complete on its own. You should not have to click anything — including anything of ours — to get the real answer.

Read the companion piece: a sourced teardown of every digital-legacy service, password manager and platform legacy-contact feature, or take the same comparison as an open dataset in JSON and CSV.

Published by CairnVault, who build a product in this category and are therefore not a neutral party — which is exactly why every claim below links to somebody else's words rather than ours. Check them. Corrections are welcome and get recorded with a date: open an issue.

This is general information about how products and processes work. It is not legal advice. Estate law is state-specific and fact-specific; talk to an attorney about your own situation.

How this was checked, and what that is worth

Claims were verified by fetching the source page directly with a browser user-agent, falling back to a rendering fetch where a site blocked plain retrieval, then to the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine where a live page could not be retrieved by either method, and to a named secondary source only where nothing else worked. Every such fallback is flagged inline in the answer itself rather than smoothed over.

Where a company appears to publish no policy at all, we say so and treat the absence as the answer. Spotify (Q30), Reddit (Q44) and Patreon (Q45) are all in that position. A family searching for those policies deserves to know the search is not their failure.

Questions 1–15, checked 2–3 August 2026

Several sites could not be retrieved at all in that pass: Instagram's help centre, Coinbase's and Kraken's deceased-account pages, and the Uniform Law Commission's own site (all JavaScript-rendered or bot-protected). We said so in the relevant answers instead of filling the gap from memory. The single weakest answer in that set is Q15 — what actually happens to an unexported vault when a password-manager company shuts down has no primary source we could find anywhere, from any vendor or regulator. That is reported as a real gap, not a formality.

Questions 16–30, checked 6–7 August 2026

Coinbase's deceased-account page could not be retrieved by any method tried, matching the result from the first batch. American Airlines' aa.com blocked every direct fetch, so that answer's quotes come from a search engine's indexed excerpt rather than a page we read ourselves. Spotify appears not to publish a dedicated policy at all. Amazon's current live help pages would not load, so that answer relies on an archived copy that may be out of date.

No claim in that batch contradicts the first fifteen or the companion teardown. If anything it reinforces the pattern: broad willingness to close an account or disburse funds to a documented executor, and zero willingness anywhere to hand over a password or a login session, no matter how complete the paperwork.

Questions 31–46, checked 7–8 August 2026

Three answers rest on Wayback Machine snapshots because the live pages returned bot-protection blocks to every direct attempt: Discord (snapshot roughly eight months old), Etsy, and both Substack articles (roughly seven weeks old). Treat those quotes as accurate as of the snapshot date rather than as of today. Coinbase (Q39) failed for a third consecutive research pass, this time by direct fetch, rendering fetch and Wayback lookup alike — the Internet Archive holds no snapshot of the page at all. That is now the most persistent gap in this library.

Two answers are deliberately narrower than their question: Q42 verifies who owns the money in a joint account but could find no bank's documented policy on restricting a joint account's access, as opposed to a sole-owner one; Q43 is sourced fully for Florida and explicitly does not claim to describe the other forty-nine states.

The most useful new pattern in this batch is that there is no industry norm at all. Ring documents a self-service ownership transfer that needs neither a death certificate nor a court order. Substack documents that no amount of paperwork will get an heir a transfer — a paying newsletter can be taken down, never handed over, and not even exported. Microsoft requires a subpoena or court order in the United States. Those are three vendors, three completely different answers, and none of them can be guessed from the others.

Q46 is different in kind from every other answer here and is worth reading with that in mind. It is not a vendor policy but a statute, it changed recently, and nearly every published fifty-state comparison of digital-asset law still gives Louisiana's repealed citation. We verified the whole chain ourselves — the 2016 enrolled bill that created the chapter, and the current statute text showing it repealed — and we also state precisely which parts of the replacement we could not confirm. This is not legal advice, and a Louisiana attorney should be the one relied on.
