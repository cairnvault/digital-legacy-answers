What happens to a life insurance policy nobody knows about?

FULLY SOURCED

This is a real, common problem with two separate official tools built specifically to solve it — one run by insurance regulators, one by state treasurers — and both are free.

The first stop is the NAIC Life Insurance Policy Locator, run by the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (the umbrella body for U.S. state insurance regulators). Per NAIC's own description: "Every year, millions of dollars in life insurance benefits remain unclaimed. Sometimes, beneficiaries can't find their deceased relative's policies or, in some cases, may not even know the policies exist. To help solve this issue, the NAIC created the Life Insurance Policy Locator, a free online tool that helps consumers find their deceased loved one's life insurance policies and annuity contracts." Source Using it means submitting the deceased's Social Security number (or ITIN), legal name, date of birth, date of death, veteran status, and your relationship to them; the request is checked against participating insurers, and the outcome is binary: "If a policy is found and you are the beneficiary, the life insurance or annuity company will contact you directly. If no policy is found or you are not the beneficiary, you will not be contacted" — so silence doesn't necessarily mean there's no policy, only that no participating company matched a claim to you specifically.

The second mechanism matters for policies that were never claimed at all, even by an insurer who tried: state unclaimed property law. The National Association of Unclaimed Property Administrators (NAUPA), the official network of state treasurers' offices, lists "Insurance payments or refunds and life insurance policies" among the common categories of property that get turned over to a state treasury when a rightful owner can't be found or doesn't come forward within a state-set dormancy period. Source NAUPA's own official search portal is unclaimed.org, which routes to each state's individual unclaimed-property database (a well-known companion is missingmoney.com, a multi-state search tool NAUPA also participates in). The practical lesson: if a policy was never located via the NAIC tool, or the insurer itself couldn't find a beneficiary, checking every state where the person ever lived or worked on the unclaimed-property registry is a second, free, independent chance to find it.

What we could not verify: the specific dormancy period (how long an unclaimed life insurance payout sits before being escheated to a state) — NAUPA's own page illustrates the general dormancy mechanic with an unrelated example (a security deposit, one-year dormancy in Tennessee), not a life-insurance-specific figure, and dormancy periods are set state by state. Widely cited figures in the 3–5-year range for life insurance specifically are plausible but not confirmed here from NAUPA's or any state's own primary text.
