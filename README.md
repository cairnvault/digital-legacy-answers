## 1. What happens to my Google account when I die?

Nothing happens automatically unless you set something up in advance. Google's mechanism is
**Inactive Account Manager (IAM)**: you choose a period of inactivity, and if your account goes
quiet for that long, Google notifies up to **10 people you designate** and can share specific data
categories with them, or simply delete the account. Per Google's own help page: "You can select up
to 10 people to receive this data" and the plan triggers "if your Google Account has been inactive
for a certain period of time" — there is **no death check of any kind**; it is a pure inactivity
timer. [Source](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/3036546?hl=en)

If you never set up IAM, your family can instead use Google's separate **deceased-user request
process**. It offers three distinct outcomes: obtaining data from the account, requesting funds
(e.g. an AdSense balance), or closing the account. Google states plainly it **"cannot provide
passwords or other login details"** — no request, however documented, gets your family your actual
password. Google also warns about sequencing: "If you select to close the Google account, Google
is unable to process any request to turn over the contents of the account at a later date" — so
closing the account forecloses any later data request; order matters.
[Source](https://support.google.com/accounts/troubleshooter/6357590?hl=en)

**What we could not verify:** Google's own static help page does not display the exact inactivity
period choices (they render inside a sign-in-gated interactive tool). Widely repeated figures like
"3 to 18 months" are plausible but not confirmed from Google's own text in this research pass —
treat any specific number range as unconfirmed.

**Bottom line:** set up Inactive Account Manager now. Without it, your family can eventually close
the account or, in limited circumstances, get some content — but never your password, and Google
decides case by case.

## 2. What happens to my Apple/iCloud account when I die? (Legacy Contact — and what it does NOT cover)

Apple's Legacy Contact is the most thorough of the big-platform tools, with one major gap. Two
things are required to use it, per Apple's support page: "Your Legacy Contact must have both the
access key and your death certificate to request access after you pass away."
[Source](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102631)

What they get once approved is broad — photos, messages, notes, files, device backups. What they
explicitly do **not** get: "your Legacy Contact can't access certain information. Inaccessible data
includes movies, music, books, or subscriptions you purchased with your Apple Account, and data
stored in your iCloud Keychain (payment information, passwords, and passkeys)." Passwords are the
one category a family settling an estate needs most, and it's the one category walled off.
[Source](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102631)

If you never set up a Legacy Contact, Apple still has a fallback: "In the unfortunate event of a
customer's death, Apple provides options for their loved ones to request access to or delete their
Apple Account." This generally requires "a death certificate, and might also require a court
order." In the U.S., a court order naming the requester as the account's rightful inheritor is
sufficient on its own — **no pre-set access key required** for this route, contrary to a claim we
previously could not verify and are not repeating.
[Source](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102431) (fetched in full, Published Date: April 02, 2026)

One more concrete fact on that same page, directly relevant if you're trying to get into a locked
phone: "devices locked with a passcode are protected by passcode encryption, and Apple can't help
remove the passcode lock without erasing the device." There is no backdoor — Apple support or a
court order can help with the Apple ID/iCloud account, but not with a passcode-locked device itself
without wiping it.

**What we could not verify:** any claim that Apple deletes the account or its data after a fixed
number of years post-legacy-access. That language does not appear on Apple's current live page and
we are not asserting it.

## 3. What happens to my Facebook/Instagram account when I die?

**Facebook** has a self-service Legacy Contact feature, limited to one person. Once your profile is
memorialized, per Meta's own help pages, that legacy contact **can**: "Write a pinned post for your
profile," "Update your profile picture and cover photo," **"Request the removal of your account,"**
and "Download a copy of what you've shared on Facebook."
[Source](https://www.facebook.com/help/1568013990080948) They explicitly **cannot**: "Log into your
account. Read your messages. Remove any of your friends or make new friend requests."
[Source](https://www.facebook.com/help/1568013990080948)

Memorialization itself (which can happen with or without a legacy contact, once Facebook is
notified of the death) changes the account this way: "The word Remembering will be shown next to
the person's name on their profile," "No one can log into a memorialized account," and "Content the
person shared (ex: photos, posts) stays on Facebook and is visible to the audience it was shared
with." A companion page adds: "In most cases, once an account is memorialized, content can't be
removed from it." [Source](https://www.facebook.com/help/1017717331640041) and
[Source](https://www.facebook.com/help/625352257502955)

So the honest summary for Facebook: a legacy contact can curate the memorial and even get the whole
account deleted, but cannot read your messages or ever log in as you, and most content becomes
frozen in place rather than editable.

**What we could not verify:** Instagram-specific mechanics. Instagram's own help center is fully
JavaScript-rendered and blocked every automated fetch attempt in this research pass (both direct
`curl` with a browser user-agent and WebFetch returned nav-only shells with no article text). I
could not confirm from Instagram's own page whether it offers an in-app Legacy Contact assignment
the way Facebook does, versus only a memorialization request form. Do not publish a specific claim
about Instagram's mechanics without a fresh, successful fetch of Instagram's own help article.

## 4. Can my spouse access my password manager if I die?

For most major password managers, yes — but only if you set it up in advance, and what your spouse
gets is a raw list of credentials, not guidance. Across the vendors with a working "emergency
access" feature (Bitwarden, LastPass, Proton Pass, NordPass, and — on the evidence we could
find — Keeper), the pattern is
consistent: your **spouse only needs a free account** with that same vendor. (Whether *you*, the
vault owner, need a paid plan to grant the feature is verified only for Bitwarden, which requires
Premium or a paid organisation; we did not confirm the grantor-side requirement vendor by vendor.) Bitwarden's documentation states it
outright: "Anyone with a free or premium Bitwarden account on the same Bitwarden server can be
designated as a trusted emergency contact."
[Source](https://bitwarden.com/help/emergency-access/)

Two important exceptions cut the other way. **1Password has no emergency-access feature at all.**
Its "Emergency Kit" is a downloadable PDF meant for self-recovery, and 1Password's own guidance is
direct: "It's important that you don't share your Emergency Kit with anyone."
[Source](https://support.1password.com/emergency-kit/) The only way a spouse gets in is if you
printed that PDF and told them where it's kept — a paper backup, not a product feature. **Dashlane's
automated emergency-contact feature was discontinued**; what remains is a manual encrypted export
file or live sharing between two already-active accounts, neither of which is triggered by death.

Critically, **none of the working emergency-access features verify that you actually died** — every
one of them is a silence timer: your spouse requests access, you have a window to decline, and if
you don't respond (because you're grieving-adjacent-to-them, on vacation, or genuinely
incapacitated), access opens automatically.

**What we could not verify:** whether Keeper's free tier can be used by the *recipient* of emergency
access — the primary documentation doesn't say, and we are not guessing.

## 5. How does Bitwarden emergency access actually work — and what triggers it?

Bitwarden's Emergency Access is a request-and-wait system, not a death-verification system. Per
Bitwarden's own documentation:

- **To grant emergency access, you (the owner) need a paid plan** — Premium, or membership in a
  paid Families/Teams/Enterprise organization.
- **To receive it, your contact only needs a Bitwarden account, and it can be free**: "Anyone with a
  free or premium Bitwarden account on the same Bitwarden server can be designated as a trusted
  emergency contact." [Source](https://bitwarden.com/help/emergency-access/)
- **You set a wait time**, with a documented minimum: "The minimum wait time is one day."
  [Source](https://bitwarden.com/help/add-and-manage-trusted-emergency-contacts/)
- During that wait window, you can approve or deny the request manually at any point.
- **If you do nothing**, access is granted automatically once the wait time elapses.

What actually triggers release, in practice: your emergency contact logs into their own Bitwarden
account, opens the Emergency Access section, and clicks "request access" against your account. That
starts the clock. There is no death certificate, ID check, or third-party attestation anywhere in
the flow — it is functionally identical whether you're deceased, in a coma, or just didn't see a
notification email while on a two-week trip.

What your contact receives once access is granted is **read access to your entire vault** (and,
depending on settings, the ability to take it over) — not a curated set of instructions. It's a
credential dump, delivered on a timer, with no third-party review at any point.

**What we could not verify:** Bitwarden's documented maximum wait time. The one-day minimum is
confirmed directly; community forum discussion (not authoritative) suggests it's an open numeric
field rather than a fixed dropdown, which would mean there's no hard maximum — but that specific
point rests on secondary discussion, not Bitwarden's own primary documentation, so treat it as
unconfirmed.

## 6. Should I write my passwords down for my family?

There's a real tension here, and most security guidance doesn't resolve it honestly.

On one side: NIST's password guidelines (SP 800-63B) explicitly list writing passwords down as a
named attack surface, categorized under "Duplication" risks: "Passwords written on paper are
disclosed." [Source](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-3/sp800-63b.html) That's a real, documented
threat model, not theoretical — a written password can be found, photographed, or stolen by anyone
who has physical access to wherever it's kept.

On the other side: the alternative most security tools point you toward — a password manager's own
emergency-access or recovery mechanism — often turns out, on inspection, to also rely on a
written/printed secret. 1Password's own recommended backstop *is* a printed document: its Emergency
Kit PDF, which 1Password says explicitly not to share with anyone digitally, while separately
being the tool most people are told to print and store physically for exactly this situation.
[Source](https://support.1password.com/emergency-kit/) In other words, even a security-first vendor's
own answer to "what if I can't log in" ends in a piece of paper in a safe.

So the honest, non-hand-wavy answer is: **it's not "write it down" vs. "don't" — it's about where.**
A password (or a password manager's master password / recovery key) taped to a monitor or saved in
an unencrypted note on your phone is a real, documented risk. The same information printed once and
locked in a fireproof safe, a bank safe-deposit box, or given to an attorney holding your estate
documents is a fundamentally different risk profile — physical, access-controlled, and not
exposed to anyone who compromises your phone or email.

**What we could not verify:** there is no single authoritative "yes, write it down" or "no, never"
guidance from a security standards body — the honest answer is genuinely a risk trade-off between
two named threat categories (digital compromise vs. physical loss/theft), not a settled rule.

## 7. Is it legal for my family to log into my accounts after I die? (US-centric; RUFADAA)

**This is general information, not legal advice — talk to an estate attorney in your state for your
specific situation.**

In the U.S., the relevant law is the **Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act
(RUFADAA)**, a model law drafted by the Uniform Law Commission that individual states then enact
(with minor variations) into their own statutes. According to Wikipedia's "Digital inheritance"
article, which cites the Uniform Law Commission as its source for adoption data, "as of 2021, 47
states have enacted the RUFADAA" — we could not independently confirm that exact count on the ULC's
own site, since uniformlaws.org is fully JavaScript-rendered and returned no retrievable article
text to automated fetching in this research pass, so treat the "47 states" figure as
secondary-sourced, not primary-confirmed.
[Source (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_inheritance) One state's actual
enactment — Florida's Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, Florida Statutes Chapter 740 — is
directly fetchable and confirms the substance of RUFADAA:
[Source (primary, Florida's enactment)](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0700-0799/0740/0740.html)

What RUFADAA actually creates is **not** a right to log in with someone's password. It creates a
three-tier priority system for a **formal disclosure request directed at the company (the
"custodian")**: (1) an online tool the user set up (like Google's Inactive Account Manager or a
platform's Legacy Contact) takes priority over everything else if it's modifiable at any time; (2) if
no online tool was used, a will, trust, or power of attorney governs; (3) absent either, the
company's terms of service control. Florida's statute states this directly: "a direction regarding
disclosure using an online tool overrides a contrary direction by the user in a will, trust, power
of attorney, or other record." [Source](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0700-0799/0740/0740.html), §740.003

Even with the right paperwork, the law only obligates the **custodian to disclose** — it is a
request process aimed at the company, with a certified death certificate, court letters, and often
a specific court finding required (Florida's §740.006 lists five separate documentation
requirements for full message content, including that disclosure "would not violate 18 U.S.C. ss.
2701 et seq." — the federal Stored Communications Act). It does not authorize simply typing in the
deceased's password.

**What we could not verify:** whether, and to what extent, logging into a deceased person's account
directly with their known password (rather than going through the formal RUFADAA request process)
could itself expose a family member to liability under a state computer-crime statute. That is a
fact-specific legal question this research pass did not chase down a primary source for — ask an
attorney.

## 8. Does my will cover my digital assets?

**This is general information, not legal advice.**

Under RUFADAA (see Q7), a will *can* direct what happens to your digital assets — but it is
explicitly **subordinate to any "online tool"** you've configured with the company itself. Florida's
enactment of the law states the hierarchy in one sentence: "If the online tool allows the user to
modify or delete a direction at all times, a direction regarding disclosure using an online tool
overrides a contrary direction by the user in a will, trust, power of attorney, or other record."
[Source](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0700-0799/0740/0740.html),
§740.003(1)

Practically, that means: if you set Google's Inactive Account Manager to notify a specific person,
that setting controls — even if your will says something different — because Google's tool lets you
change your mind at any time and the law treats that as the more current expression of your wishes.
Your will only becomes the controlling instrument for a given service if you **never used that
service's own online tool**.

There's a second limitation worth naming plainly: even when your will *is* the controlling document,
it does not hand your executor your password. It authorizes them to make a **formal request to the
company**, per §740.005–740.008 of the same statute, which the company can fulfill with full
account access, partial access, or merely a copy of specific data — the company's discretion, not
automatic. A will that says "give my executor my Instagram" is a valid instruction under the law,
but the mechanism by which it's honored is still a request the platform processes, with its own
documentation requirements (death certificate, letters of administration, sometimes a specific
court finding) — not a login.

The takeaway: a will is a real, legally meaningful piece of the puzzle, but by itself it is
**necessary but not sufficient**. It does nothing for any account where you've already set up (or
could set up) that service's own legacy tool, and even where it does apply, it starts a formal
request process rather than handing over credentials.

**What we could not verify:** state-by-state variation in exactly which documents a custodian can
demand — this research pass only confirmed Florida's specific list; other RUFADAA-enacting states
may require different combinations.

## 9. What happens to my crypto if I die?

This splits cleanly into two very different situations, and almost every general "digital legacy"
answer glosses over the difference.

**If your crypto is self-custodied** — a hardware wallet, a software wallet, or just a seed phrase
written down — **there is no company involved at all**. Whoever has the seed phrase or private key
controls the funds; there is no support line, no death certificate process, no "forgot password"
flow, because there is no central party who could reset anything. Bitcoin.org's own educational
page states this in the plainest possible terms: "Your bitcoins can be lost forever if you don't
have a backup plan for your peers and family. If the location of your wallets or your passwords are
not known by anyone when you are gone, there is no hope that your funds will ever be recovered."
[Source](https://bitcoin.org/en/secure-your-wallet) This is not a policy choice any company could
change even if it wanted to — it's how public-key cryptography works. If nobody you trust knows
where your seed phrase is, or how to use it, self-custodied crypto is permanently lost the moment
you are, regardless of what your will says.

**If your crypto sits on a custodial exchange** (Coinbase, Kraken, etc.) instead, the situation is
structurally more like a brokerage account: the exchange, not you, holds the keys, so in principle
an estate can request access the same way it would for a bank account — with a death certificate
and court-issued letters of administration. However, **we could not verify the specific procedure
of any individual exchange in this research pass**: both Coinbase's and Kraken's deceased-account
documentation returned bot-protection pages (Cloudflare challenge / no retrievable content) to
every automated fetch attempt, so we are not citing a specific exchange's process here. If you hold
crypto on an exchange, check that exchange's own current support documentation directly, or contact
their support, rather than assuming it works like your bank.

**What we could not verify:** any specific exchange's actual documented death/estate-access
procedure. Treat this as an open item requiring a manual check per exchange.

## 10. What happens to my subscriptions and recurring charges when I die?

Nothing stops automatically. A subscription is a standing instruction to a card or bank account,
and it keeps charging until either (a) the subscription itself is canceled, or (b) the payment
method behind it stops working (the card is closed, the bank account is frozen).

Some routes for stopping platform-native subscriptions are documented directly by the platforms
themselves. Closing an Apple Account (available through Apple's deceased-account request process,
see Q2/Q14) would stop Apple-billed subscriptions — App Store, iCloud+, Apple Music, etc. — tied to
that account. [Source](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102431) Google's deceased-user process
similarly offers account closure as one of its three outcomes, which would stop Google-billed
subscriptions (YouTube Premium, Google One, etc.).
[Source](https://support.google.com/accounts/troubleshooter/6357590?hl=en) In both cases, closing
the account is described as effectively final — Google specifically warns that closing forecloses
any later request for the account's contents, so closing should generally be the *last* step, after
data has been retrieved, not the first.

Third-party subscriptions billed directly to a credit or debit card (streaming services, apps,
memberships, anything not billed through Apple or Google's own store) are a different problem
entirely: closing the deceased's Apple or Google account does nothing to stop them. The practical
path is the same one used for any other debt or recurring charge in an estate: notify the bank or
card issuer of the death (which typically freezes or closes the card, stopping future charges),
and separately review recent statements — paper or digital — to identify what's still charging, so
each vendor can be canceled directly.

**What we could not verify:** we were not able to locate a single authoritative government or
consumer-protection source (FTC and CFPB pages we attempted to fetch on this specific topic
returned 404) laying out a definitive, step-by-step legal process for canceling a deceased person's
subscriptions. The guidance above reflects how account closure and card cancellation mechanically
work, verified against Apple's and Google's own documentation, rather than a single canonical
"what to do" checklist from a regulator.

## 11. How do I make sure my family can find my accounts without exposing them while I'm alive?

The tools that exist for this generally fall into two categories, and understanding the difference
matters.

**Category one: the platform's own "legacy" tool**, set up in advance, that only activates later.
Google's Inactive Account Manager and Apple's Legacy Contact are both this shape — you configure
them once, they sit dormant, and nothing is shared with anyone until the trigger condition (a set
period of inactivity, or your death being reported with a certificate) is met. Crucially, under
RUFADAA, using one of these tools is also the legally strongest option in the U.S.: an online tool's
instructions **override** a contrary instruction in your will, precisely because you can change
your mind on it at any time. [Source: Florida's RUFADAA enactment,
§740.003](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0700-0799/0740/0740.html)
Nothing is exposed while you're alive — that's the entire design of these tools.

**Category two: password-manager emergency access**, set up the same way — dormant until your
contact requests it and a wait period elapses. As covered in Q4/Q5, this doesn't expose anything
while you're alive either; your emergency contact cannot see your vault until they actively request
access and you fail to decline within the window you set.

What neither category solves on its own is **discovery** — your family still has to know *which*
services you use in the first place, since none of these tools are indexed anywhere central. The
practical, low-exposure answer is to maintain a list of *which accounts exist* (not the passwords
themselves) somewhere your family can find after the fact — combined with configuring the
platform-native legacy tools above for the accounts that support them, since those require no
separate document at all if the family knows to look for them.

**What we could not verify:** no single Google/Apple/Meta source documents "how to build a full list
of every account you use" — that step is inherently manual and outside any one vendor's
documentation.

## 12. What is a digital executor and do I need one?

"Digital executor" is a term you'll see constantly in estate-planning blogs and marketing — but it
is **not a term used in the law itself**. We checked the definitions section of Florida's enactment
of RUFADAA (Florida Statutes §740.002) directly, and "digital executor" does not appear anywhere in
it. [Source](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/Statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0700-0799/0740/0740.html)
The actual legal terms are:

- **"Fiduciary"** — "an original, additional, or successor personal representative, guardian,
  agent, or trustee." This is usually the same person who is already your general executor,
  guardian, or agent under a power of attorney — the law does not create a separate, distinct role
  just for digital assets.
- **"Designated recipient"** — "a person chosen by a user through an online tool to administer
  digital assets of the user." This is whoever you name in a platform's own legacy tool (Google's
  Inactive Account Manager contact, Apple's Legacy Contact, etc.).

So "do I need a digital executor" is really two separate, more precise questions. First: does your
general executor (the fiduciary named in your will) know your digital assets exist and have a way
to act on them? If not, that's a gap regardless of what you call the role. Second: have you used
each platform's own "designated recipient" tool where one exists, since — per Q7/Q8 — that
instruction legally overrides a contrary one in your will?

In practice, some people do formally name a separate, tech-comfortable person to handle the digital
side specifically, distinct from their general executor, because the tasks (closing accounts,
canceling subscriptions, retrieving photos) are different in kind from probate work. That's a
reasonable practical choice — just be clear it's an informal division of labor you're creating, not
a role the law separately defines or requires.

**What we could not verify:** whether any state's RUFADAA enactment (as opposed to Florida's
specifically, which we checked) uses "digital executor" as a defined term — we checked one state's
statute, not all of them.

## 13. What happens to my photos when I die?

It depends entirely on where the photos live, and the platforms treat this very differently from
each other.

**Apple/iCloud Photos** are explicitly *not* in the category Apple's Legacy Contact excludes. Recall
from Q2: the exclusion list is "movies, music, books, or subscriptions you purchased with your
Apple Account, and data stored in your iCloud Keychain (payment information, passwords, and
passkeys)" — photos aren't on that list, so a Legacy Contact who has the access key and a death
certificate can retrieve them. [Source](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102631)

**Google Photos**, similarly, is one of the product categories Inactive Account Manager can share:
Google's help page lists "Photos" among the exportable categories in its general data-export tool
(Takeout), and IAM is built to hand a designated contact a download link for exactly this kind of
data once triggered.
[Source](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/3024190) You can also proactively export your
own Google Photos library at any time via Google Takeout, while you're alive, as a backup — that's
a self-service tool for the account owner, not a survivor-access mechanism, but it's worth knowing
about for your own redundancy.

**Facebook/Instagram photos**: per Q3, once memorialized, "Content the person shared (ex: photos,
posts) stays on Facebook and is visible to the audience it was shared with" — the photos aren't
deleted, but "in most cases, once an account is memorialized, content can't be removed from it," and
a legacy contact's *only* download mechanism is the general "Download a copy of what you've shared
on Facebook" action, not selective photo retrieval.
[Source](https://www.facebook.com/help/1017717331640041)

**What we could not verify:** whether Google's Inactive Account Manager, in practice, delivers a
full-resolution photo library or a compressed/limited export — the help documentation confirms
Photos as a shareable category but doesn't specify format or quality for the IAM-triggered share
specifically (as opposed to a manual Takeout export, which does specify format details).

## 14. My parent died and I can't get into their phone/accounts — what can I actually do?

Start by separating the **device** from the **account** — they require different processes, and
conflating them wastes time.

**If the phone itself is passcode-locked**, Apple is direct about this: "devices locked with a
passcode are protected by passcode encryption, and Apple can't help remove the passcode lock
without erasing the device." [Source](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102431) There is no support
process, no death certificate, and no court order that gets you past a passcode without wiping the
device and losing what's on it — this is a hard technical limit, not a policy Apple could waive.
Before you consider erasing it, check the obvious: a written note, a password manager on another
device, or a family member the deceased may have shared their passcode or Face ID with while alive.

**For the Apple Account (iCloud) itself** — separate from the physical device — Apple's fallback
process (if no Legacy Contact was set up in advance) requires "a death certificate, and might also
require a court order." In the U.S., a court order naming you as the rightful inheritor of the
account is generally sufficient. [Source](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102431) This can get you account-level access (photos,
notes, files) even without ever touching the locked device, since iCloud data is separately stored.

**For a Google account**, Google's deceased-user process can, "in certain circumstances," provide
content from the account or close it — but explicitly states it "cannot provide passwords or other
login details" under any circumstance.
[Source](https://support.google.com/accounts/troubleshooter/6357590?hl=en)

**For a Microsoft account**, the bar is the highest of the major platforms: "Microsoft must first be
formally served with a valid subpoena or court order to consider whether it is able to lawfully
release a deceased or incapacitated user's information," and even then, "sending a request or
providing a subpoena or court order does not guarantee that we will be able to assist you."
[Source](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/account-billing/accessing-outlook-com-onedrive-and-other-microsoft-services-when-someone-has-died-ebbd2860-917e-4b39-9913-212362da6b2f)
There are narrow documentation-only exceptions in Germany and China, but not in the U.S.

**Practically**: gather the death certificate first — every path above needs it. Then check whether
any legacy/inactive-account tool was already configured (that's the fast path if so). If not,
expect the process to take real time and, for anything beyond basic account closure, likely a court
order.

**What we could not verify:** the specific timeline any of these companies typically takes to
process such a request — none of the pages fetched state a service-level expectation.

## 15. What happens to my password manager account if the company shuts down?

There is no universal guarantee here, and this is the one question in this set where we found the
least direct, company-specific documentation.

What we could confirm is the **individual-level safeguard**: most password managers let you export
your own vault at any time, while the company is still operating, specifically so you are not
dependent on the company's continued existence. Bitwarden's documentation, for example, describes
exporting to `.csv` or `.json` formats and states: "No unencrypted data is transferred over the
internet, because data is decrypted locally by the client before exporting."
[Source](https://bitwarden.com/help/export-your-data/) That's a safeguard you control now, in
advance — not something that helps if you never used it and the company disappears later.

For EU residents specifically, there is a **regulatory backstop** that doesn't exist in the same
form in the U.S.: GDPR's right to data portability. The regulation's text states a data subject
"shall have the right to receive the personal data concerning him or her... in a structured,
commonly used and machine-readable format." [Source](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-20-gdpr/) That's a
legal right to get your data out of a company while it's a going concern — it does not by itself
specify what happens in an actual bankruptcy or unplanned shutdown, and it is EU-specific, not a
U.S. protection.

**What I could not verify — and this is a genuine, not just formal, gap**: we could not find a
primary source (from any vendor, regulator, or a documented historical case) describing exactly
what happens to an *existing, unexported* vault when a password manager company itself shuts down
or is acquired — whether there's a standard wind-down notice period, whether data is transferred to
an acquirer, deleted, or held. This varies by company and by the circumstances of the shutdown
(voluntary wind-down vs. bankruptcy vs. acquisition), and none of the vendor documentation checked
in this research pass makes a forward-looking promise about it. The only reliable protection we
found evidence for is exporting your own vault proactively, before you'd ever need to rely on the
company's behavior in a shutdown scenario.
